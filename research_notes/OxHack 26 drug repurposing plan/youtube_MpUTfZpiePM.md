## 2. What is the main content from ~17:08 onward, and how does it fit the overall talk structure?

### Takeaway
17:08 falls at the end of the clinical-trials segment. The rest of the talk covers:
- the last two value-chain stages, manufacturing/supply chain and regulatory submissions, including the remark that FDA "last month" started using AI to review dossiers;
- a "Key challenges in AI Implementation" wrap-up, which contains the talk's most quotable caution: AI augments but "cannot just do the work and give us the drug", and LLM tools invent references and journals;
- about 8 minutes of Q&A covering academia, toxicology and pharmacovigilance, metrics for judging AI, LLM literature review, and regulation.

  - **Possible citation mismatch on the screening slide:** The superscripts tie Gómez-Bombarelli et al. 2018 (which, as I recall it, is a VAE molecule-design paper) to "GANs", and Kearnes et al. 2016 (a graph-convolution paper) to "VAEs". If so, the citations are shifted by one. That small mismatch echoes the speaker's own warning that citations need checking.
- None of the tools in the team's stack is endorsed or criticised in the talk: Open Targets, ChEMBL, PubChem, RCSB PDB, DiffDock, Vina and Snowflake Cortex are all absent. Any link to them is the team's own framing.

## 4. What does the speaker say about what works, what fails, and how to evaluate AI for drug discovery?

### Takeaway
**What works, in his view:** AI as *augmentation* at every stage. Examples are omics integration, drug–target interaction prediction, ADMET and toxicity prediction, biomarker stratification, protocol and document drafting with QC, and automatic safety-signal detection.

**What fails or is risky:** expecting AI to deliver a drug end to end; legacy integration and siloed or poorly curated data; black-box models; and LLM hallucination (invented references, wrong journal names). He and his colleagues do not rely on the output of multiple LLMs alone.

**How to evaluate:** overall cost, time and attrition, plus per-stage metrics such as hit rate, screening cost, lead-optimization time and success rate. His ultimate test is whether innovative medicines reach patients faster, and he says the evidence is "too early" to judge.

### Cited Findings
- **Works, as augmentation:** "AI can augment the process. AI can support us with each of the value chain… However, AI cannot just do the work and give us the drug that we would need." — [Video 19:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1167s)
- **Where he sees AI helping:**
  - Omics integration gives "a holistic view of the disease mechanism".
  - With large datasets "the prediction accuracy is increasing".
  - ML predicts drug–target interactions and is "improving the hit rate and also reducing the cost".
  
  — [Video 06:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=391s); [Video 07:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=470s)
- **Safety:** In silico toxicology "predicts the toxicological profiles based on the chemical structures" and reduces "reliance on animal testing". Live algorithms detect pharmacovigilance signals automatically. — [Video 11:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=700s); [Video 23:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1419s)
- **Fails: hallucination.** "Sometime they're giving us references [that don't] exist, sometimes making up journals which never exist." In Q&A he adds "giving you the right paper title but… a different journal's name", and "you should not solely rely on LLMs to provide you the knowledge mining". — [Video 20:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1222s); [Video 26:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1613s)
- **Fails: multiple LLMs are not a safeguard by themselves.** "I'm just using multiple LLM models and using solely that particular output — I'm being very honest, I don't do that, and my team and my colleagues across the industry [don't]." — [Video 26:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1594s)
- **What improves accuracy:** "If you train your algorithm, fine-tune it with your own data sets, then we see the accuracy." — [Video 27:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1638s)
- **Fails: integration and data.** He cites legacy systems, data spread across countries and local regulations. The slide adds that "up to 70% data used in AI models may be unstructured or poorly curated" and that complex models act as "black boxes". — [Video 19:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1193s); [slide, Video 19:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1170s)
- **Fails: where drugs fail.** The slide says "up to 80% of drugs failing in clinical trials due to efficacy or safety concerns", citing Paul et al. 2010. He also says "working is not good enough. It should work better than the standard of care". — [Video 10:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=653s); [Video 11:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=664s)
- **Decision rule:** "The sooner you can kill a molecule is better", because a Phase 3 failure costs "[$300–]500 million at least". — [Video 10:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=615s)
- **Evaluation metrics:**
  - Overall: cost, timeline and success or attrition rate.
  - Per stage: hit rate and screening cost; lead-optimization time and success rate.
  - Ultimate: "for a patient, is it bringing in innovative medicine to the market faster".
  - Status of the evidence: "some data being published but… it's a bit too early. We need to wait… and see the robustness of the data."
  
  — [Video 24:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1444s); [Video 25:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1519s)
- **External cross-check of the failure split:** Sun et al. (2022, *Acta Pharm Sin B*) attribute clinical failures about 40–50% to lack of efficacy, about 30% to unmanageable toxicity, 10–15% to poor drug-like properties and about 10% to commercial or strategic issues. Efficacy plus toxicity (about 70–80%) is consistent with the slide's "up to 80%… efficacy or safety". — [ASBMB Today (Sun)](https://www.asbmb.org/asbmb-today/opinions/031222/90-of-drugs-fail-clinical-trials); [DOAJ record, Sun et al. 2022](https://doaj.org/article/c83f55dcfaec4f418216ac4875b7cd5d)
- **Source-quality warning:** The AI Summit's own 22 Sep 2025 write-up attributes quotes to Bose that do not appear in the recorded talk. Examples are "with unprecedented accuracy" and "slashing the time needed to screen millions of compounds from years to weeks"; neither "unprecedented", "slashing" nor "weeks" occurs in the transcript. Quote him only from video timestamps. — [AI Summit article](https://newyork.theaisummit.com/latest-news/ai-in-drug-discovery-and-development-accelerating-pharmaceutical-innovation/); [Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM)

### Inferences
- Taken together, his stance means that agreement among several LLMs is not evidence. A model jury is defensible only if it is (a) restricted to evidence fetched from the source databases, (b) checked by code for citation integrity, and (c) advisory, with a human making the call.
- His evaluation framing (per-stage, measurable proxies with the ultimate outcome deferred) maps neatly onto a hackathon app. It can measure its own stage metrics but should not claim downstream impact.
- His statistics are framed as industry-wide ("all data I'm giving you is across the industry"), and the slides cite older literature (Paul 2010, Wong 2019). Treat them as widely quoted ballpark figures, not as fresh measurements. [Video 11:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=718s)

### Gaps
- He gives no measured effect sizes for AI in drug discovery, such as improvement in hit rate or reduction in time.
- He gives no guidance on validating generative or LLM systems beyond "don't rely solely" and "fine-tune with your own data".
- He says nothing about docking, structure-based methods or repurposing-specific validation.

## 5. Which ideas should our app adopt or avoid, and which would make a stronger demo or pitch?

### Takeaway
Adopt the talk's three guardrails as explicit features:
1. AI augments and the student decides.
2. Never display an LLM-generated reference: every claim must resolve to a record fetched from Open Targets, ChEMBL, PubChem or PDB.
3. Measure with explicit stage metrics, and design to "kill" weak candidates early.

For the pitch, open with his funnel numbers: repurposing starts from drugs that have already cleared human safety testing. Present the multi-LLM jury as a transparent triage tool whose answers are tied to the fetched evidence, not as an oracle.

### Cited Findings
These are the talk's anchors for the recommendations; the recommendations themselves are in Inferences.
- AI cannot "give us the drug"; it augments. — [Video 19:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1167s)
- LLMs fabricate references and journals; he does not rely solely on outputs from multiple LLMs. — [Video 20:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1222s); [Video 26:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1594s)
- Kill molecules early; a drug must beat the standard of care. — [Video 10:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=615s); [Video 11:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=664s)
- Multiple pathophysiologies mean multiple targets; about 30% of failures trace back to inadequate target validation. — [Video 03:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=182s); [Video 04:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=282s)
- Data silos, black boxes, and up to 70% of data unstructured or poorly curated. — [slide, Video 19:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1170s)
- Metrics: cost, time, attrition, hit rate, and "too early" for robust impact data. — [Video 24:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1444s)
- Academia is the "engine room" for disease mechanism and compound screening. — [Video 21:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1291s)
- Pharmacovigilance-style automatic signal detection is a valuable use of AI. — [Video 23:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1419s)
- It is a regulated industry. — [Video 28:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpUTfZpiePM&t=1716s)

### Inferences

#### Adopt: model jury (Snowflake Cortex)
1. **Jury works only from fetched evidence.** Give every juror the same JSON evidence bundle: Ensembl target IDs and Open Targets association scores per data type, ChEMBL molecule IDs, mechanism of action, max phase, pChEMBL activities, PubChem CIDs and PDB IDs. Require each reason to cite bundle IDs, and have code strip or flag any ID not in the bundle. Never render LLM-written paper titles, journals or DOIs. This answers his "right title, wrong journal" anecdote directly.
2. **Show disagreement, but don't present agreement as confidence.** Report the spread of rankings across jurors, for example Kendall's W across juror rankings plus each candidate's rank range. Label it "consensus on the same evidence", not "likelihood of working", because he explicitly does not rely on outputs from several LLMs alone.
3. **Add a non-LLM baseline ("juror zero").** Use a deterministic score, for example target–disease association × drug approval status × potency from measured bioactivity. Highlight where the LLM jury departs from it. This addresses the "black box" interpretability challenge.
4. **Ask for reasons to kill, not just reasons to pursue.** Each juror should give pros, cons and "what evidence would change my mind". This mirrors "the sooner you can kill a molecule the better".
5. **Make the human decision explicit.** The student picks the drug and structure, and the UI states that the jury is advisory.

#### Adopt: evidence and biology framing
6. **Show the standard of care.** List drugs already approved or used for the disease, so candidates are judged against what exists ("better than the standard of care"). In the COVID-19 and myeloma demos this also shows the app understands the field.
7. **Show several targets per disease.** Break each target's support down by evidence type, so the strength of target validation is visible. This answers "30% fail at target validation" and "multiple pathophysiologies".
8. **Show provenance and data-quality badges on every card.** Include the source, release version and fetch time. Make "no bioactivity found" and other gaps explicit rather than letting the LLM paper over them. This answers the "up to 70% unstructured or poorly curated" point.
9. **Add a known-safety panel.** Approved drugs come with human safety data, so show warnings, withdrawal or boxed-warning status and adverse-event signals. This ties to his pharmacovigilance answer. The team must check that the current Open Targets or ChEMBL schema exposes these fields; I did not verify this.

#### Adopt: docking and evaluation
10. **Present docking as a plausibility check, not efficacy evidence.** Show DiffDock or Vina results as a "binding plausibility" view, with measured ChEMBL bioactivity listed first. Docking cannot address efficacy or safety, which drive up to about 80% of clinical failures (his slide; consistent with Sun et al.).
11. **Run the known-answer check honestly.** Keep the baricitinib/COVID-19 label, but avoid leakage. If ranking evidence includes drug–disease clinical precedence (known-drug or clinical evidence linking baricitinib to COVID-19), the "check" is circular. Report baricitinib's rank both with and without that evidence type.
12. **Add a stage-metrics panel in his vocabulary.** Suggested metrics:
    - time to shortlist (minutes, against a manual baseline)
    - a "hit-rate" proxy: the share of top-k candidates with independent clinical or literature support
    - jury agreement
    - evidence coverage
    - cost per run (Cortex credits and GPU minutes)
    
    State plainly that downstream impact is unproven ("too early", his words).
13. **Ground the models; don't fine-tune.** His "fine-tune with your own data" advice is not feasible in 24 hours. Grounding in curated, structured data is the hackathon equivalent; say so in Q&A if judges ask.

#### Avoid
- Saying "AI finds a cure" or "predicts efficacy".
- LLM-generated literature summaries with references.
- Treating jury unanimity as validation.
- Implying a docking score equals therapeutic benefit.
- Any patient-level data. Add an "educational/research use only, not medical advice" disclaimer ("regulated industry").
- Spending time on the manufacturing or regulatory-writing parts of his talk; they are out of scope.
- Quoting the AI Summit write-up; quote only from video timestamps.

#### Pitch and demo ideas
- **Hook:** Use his funnel: 4 million compounds to 1 approval, about $2.6B, 10–15 years, under 10% success, and a Phase 3 failure costing hundreds of millions. Then: "Repurposing starts at the right-hand end of that funnel. These drugs already cleared human safety testing, so the open question is efficacy in a new disease, and that's what our app helps students explore." One caveat applies: a new indication may need different doses, so safety is not automatic.
- **Design principle on a slide:** Use his line "AI cannot just do the work and give us the drug" (Bayer VP, AI Summit London 2025, 19:45). Show it next to the citation validator.
- **Live demo moment:** A juror cites an ID or paper that is not in the evidence bundle, and the UI flags it in red. This mirrors his "right title, wrong journal" story and is memorable and easy to explain.
- **Academic angle:** He says pharma innovation mostly comes from academia and that AI most affects academic target ID and screening. That fits an Oxford student tool for early-stage hypothesis generation.
- **Sponsor ties (inference):**
  - Snowflake: a single governed evidence store plus the Cortex jury addresses his "data silos / integration" challenge.
  - Tiger Data: experiment history serves as an audit trail and reproducibility record, answering "interpretability" and "regulated industry".
  - GPU sponsor (e.g., Vultr): on-demand docking compute.
- **Quant-minded judges (HRT):** Concrete metrics, rank-agreement statistics and a leakage-controlled known-answer test are likely to land better than qualitative claims. This is an inference, not from the talk.

### Gaps
- The talk does not address:
  - drug repurposing specifically;
  - choosing or preparing protein structures;
  - docking validity;
  - multi-LLM ensembles as a method (beyond his personal practice);
  - how to score LLM outputs.

  The recommendations above extend his principles; he did not prescribe them.
- I did not verify whether Open Targets GraphQL still exposes drug warnings and adverse-event signals, or what the "known drug / clinical precedence" evidence type is currently called. The team should confirm both against the live schema before relying on recommendations 9 and 11.
