Section 1

How a drug is found

Most drugs are small molecules that stick to one particular protein in your body and change what that protein does.

Many diseases involve a protein doing too much, too little, or the wrong thing. The job is to find a molecule that fixes that protein's behaviour, gets to where it needs to go in the body, and doesn't harm you on the way. The protein you aim at is called the target.

A drug candidate has to do three things at once, and they often conflict:

  • Potency: bind the target strongly, at a low dose.
  • Selectivity: leave the other 20,000 or so human proteins alone.
  • ADMET: be absorbed, reach the right tissue, get broken down and cleared at a sensible rate, and not be toxic. (More in 3.5.)
1.2

The pipeline

Discovery runs in a fixed order, because each stage costs more than the one before. You want to kill bad candidates as early and as cheaply as possible.

The most important loop is inside lead optimization. Chemists design a variant of the molecule, make it, test it, and analyze the result. This is the design-make-test-analyze (DMTA) cycle. Each lap takes weeks, and a program can take dozens of laps. A lot of AI work tries to make those laps faster, or to need fewer of them.

The headline cost is mostly the cost of the failures, not the cost of the winner. And it has been getting worse. This is known as Eroom's law (Moore's law spelled backwards): for decades, each billion dollars of R&D produced fewer new drugs, even as the tools got better.

Why do 9 in 10 drugs fail once they reach people? The biggest reason is that the drug hit its target, but hitting that target did not help the disease enough. That is a biology failure, not a chemistry failure. Keep it in mind when judging AI claims later.

Section 2

Kinds of drugs

"Drug" covers very different kinds of things. Their sizes span more than four orders of magnitude, and AI helps each kind differently.

Most AI chemistry work

Small molecules

Classic pills. Small enough to get into cells and survive the gut. The best-measured kind of drug, with decades of public data.

e.g. aspirin, statins, imatinib

Biologics

Antibodies

Large Y-shaped proteins, given by injection. Very specific, but only reach targets on the outside of cells. A huge share of modern drug sales.

e.g. trastuzumab (Herceptin), adalimumab (Humira)

In between

Peptides

Short chains of amino acids. Bigger and more specific than small molecules, cheaper to make than antibodies.

e.g. semaglutide (Ozempic), insulin

Instructions, not binders

Nucleic acids

Deliver genetic instructions instead of a molecule that binds something. mRNA makes a protein; siRNA and antisense switch one off.

e.g. mRNA vaccines, patisiran

Living medicines

Cell and gene therapy

Re-engineer a patient's own cells (CAR-T), or deliver a working copy of a gene using a harmless virus shell (AAV).

e.g. CAR-T for leukaemia, Zolgensma

Newer mechanism

Protein degraders

Small molecules that mark a protein for the cell to destroy, instead of just blocking it. This opens up targets that have no usable pocket.

e.g. PROTACs, molecular glues

AI has the most traction with small molecules, because there are decades of measured data to learn from, and with antibodies and other proteins, because protein structure prediction now works well. These are the two areas this primer focuses on.

3.1

Target identification: picking the protein

This is the highest-leverage decision in the whole pipeline. Pick the wrong target and every later step is wasted, however well it's done. Remember the efficacy failures in 1.3? Most of them come back to the target.

How many proteins are candidates?

Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes. Only a fraction of their proteins can plausibly be reached by a drug (the "druggable genome"), and fewer still have an approved drug today.

What kinds of proteins become targets?

Most approved drugs hit one of a handful of protein families. Where a protein sits in the cell matters: antibodies can only reach things on the outside, while small molecules can get inside.

The biggest family

GPCRs (G-protein-coupled receptors)

Sit in the cell membrane and pass signals from hormones and neurotransmitters inward. About a third of all approved drugs act on one.

e.g. semaglutide → GLP-1 receptor (diabetes, obesity) · propranolol → beta-adrenergic receptor (heart) · antihistamines → histamine H1 receptor

Cancer workhorses

Kinases

Enzymes that switch other proteins on by tagging them with phosphate. When one gets stuck "on", cells can grow out of control.

e.g. imatinib → BCR-ABL (leukaemia, 2001) · osimertinib → EGFR (lung cancer)

Electrical signalling

Ion channels

Pores that open and close to let charged atoms (sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride) through. They run nerves, muscles and the heartbeat.

e.g. lidocaine → sodium channels (local anaesthetic) · amlodipine → calcium channels (blood pressure) · ivacaftor → CFTR (cystic fibrosis)

Hormone sensors

Nuclear receptors

Sit inside the cell, bind a hormone, and switch genes on or off. A small family, but it accounts for a large number of drugs.

e.g. tamoxifen → estrogen receptor (breast cancer) · dexamethasone → glucocorticoid receptor (inflammation)

Chemistry machines

Other enzymes

Proteins that speed up a specific chemical reaction. Block the enzyme and you block the reaction.

e.g. statins → HMG-CoA reductase (cholesterol) · aspirin → COX enzymes (pain) · HIV protease inhibitors

Pumps

Transporters

Move molecules across membranes. Block the pump and the molecule builds up on one side, or stops arriving on the other.

e.g. SSRIs → serotonin transporter (depression) · omeprazole → stomach proton pump (acid reflux)

The frontier

"Undruggable" targets

Transcription factors, smooth protein surfaces, and proteins that act by sticking to other proteins often have no pocket to bind. KRAS, a cancer driver, was called undruggable for about 40 years until sotorasib was approved in 2021 for one mutant form. Degraders, peptides and AI-designed binders are all aimed at this group.

What makes a good target?

TestThe question
Causal linkDoes changing this protein cause a change in the disease, or does it just correlate with it? Human genetic evidence is the strongest proof. Drug programs whose target has genetic support are about 2.6 times more likely to reach approval (Minikel et al. 2024).
DruggableIs there a pocket a small molecule can bind? Or is the protein on the cell surface, where an antibody can reach it?
Safe to changeWill blocking it also break something essential in healthy tissue? Where else in the body is it made?
MeasurableCan you build a lab test for it, and a biomarker to show the drug is working in patients?

Where AI comes in

  • Knowledge graphs. Link millions of facts (gene A is linked to disease B, drug C inhibits protein D) and predict missing links, i.e. targets nobody has tested yet.
  • Genetics at scale. Find which genetic variants are linked to a disease in data from hundreds of thousands of people (GWAS), then work out which gene each variant actually affects.
  • Single-cell data. Compare which genes are switched on in individual diseased and healthy cells, to find the cell type and gene that matter.
  • CRISPR screens. Learn from experiments that knock out each gene in turn (for example in DepMap, which shows which genes cancer cells can't survive without).
  • LLM literature mining. Read the papers no human team has time for, pull out claims with their evidence, and point out contradictions.
3.2

Protein structure prediction

To design a drug for a pocket, you need to know the pocket's shape. Measuring a protein's 3D structure in the lab can take months or years. AlphaFold2 (2020) predicts it from the amino acid sequence, often as accurately as an experiment. It is the clearest AI success in the field, and won a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Newer co-folding models (AlphaFold 3, and open alternatives such as Boltz and Chai) predict the protein together with a drug bound to it. That bound state is what drug design actually needs.

Don't overclaimA predicted structure is one frozen snapshot. Real proteins flex, and pockets can open and close. Predictions are often not precise enough for the fine-grained energy calculations drug design relies on.
3.3

Virtual screening: testing molecules in software

Instead of physically testing a million compounds with robots, test them on a computer first. Docking places each molecule into the pocket in many different poses and scores how well it fits. It is cheap per molecule. The catch is that docking scores track real binding strength only loosely, so many top-scoring molecules don't actually bind in the lab.

The modern twist is scale. Suppliers now offer catalogues of tens of billions of molecules that haven't been made yet but can be made to order. Docking every one is too slow, so teams use machine learning as a cheap first filter:

Where AI comes in

Surrogate models learn to imitate docking scores, then rank the whole catalogue in hours. ML scoring functions try to predict real binding strength from structure. Co-folding models (3.2) can stand in for docking altogether.

Generative design: inventing new molecules

Instead of searching a catalogue, a model proposes molecules that have never existed. First, a model needs a way to "see" a molecule. Here are the four common ways to represent one, all for aspirin:

Generation is just the first step of a loop. The model proposes thousands of molecules, filters discard most of them, and the best survivors steer the next round. Only a shortlist ever goes to the lab.

Two things make this harder than it sounds:

  • It optimizes many goals at once. Potency, selectivity, solubility, stability, no heart toxicity, and it has to be possible to make. Improving one usually makes another worse. A model that optimizes only binding strength produces molecules no one would take as a pill.
  • It has to be makeable. Models happily propose molecules no chemist can actually make. Practical systems score how easy a molecule is to make, or only build molecules from known reactions.

Where AI comes in

Generators: language models on SMILES, reinforcement learning (REINVENT), graph models, and diffusion models that build a molecule directly inside the 3D pocket. Retrosynthesis models work backwards from a molecule to a step-by-step recipe that starts from chemicals you can buy (AiZynthFinder, ASKCOS). LLM agents can run the whole loop, calling each tool in turn.

The honest limitYou can generate molecules in an afternoon. You can't prove any of them work without making them and testing them in a lab. Present generated molecules as leads to test, never as discoveries.

Where AI comes in

This is classic supervised learning: predict a number from a structure. People have done it since the 1960s under the name QSAR. Modern versions use graph neural networks and molecule transformers pretrained on millions of structures. Free pretrained options include ADMET-AI and the benchmark datasets in Therapeutics Data Commons. It works best for properties with plenty of training data, such as solubility, and worst for rare kinds of toxicity.

3.6

Protein and antibody design

This is arguably where AI is furthest ahead of the alternatives. Tools from David Baker's lab (RFdiffusion, ProteinMPNN) design entirely new proteins that bind a chosen target. When these designs are made and tested, a useful share of them work. Protein language models (the ESM family) learn from protein sequences the way GPT-style models learn from text.

For antibodies, AI is used to redesign the binding tips so they grip harder, make antibodies from animals safer for humans, and predict whether an antibody can be manufactured at scale without clumping.

3.7

Clinical trials: where the money actually goes

Often overlooked, because this is where most of the time and cost goes. One big idea is patient stratification. Many trials fail because a drug works very well for a subgroup, and that effect gets diluted by everyone else.

Where AI comes in

  • Biomarker discovery: find the signal, such as a gene, a scan feature or a blood marker, that predicts who will respond.
  • Trial design and recruitment: predict which hospitals will enroll patients fastest, and simulate outcomes before the trial starts.
  • External control arms: use real-world health records as a comparison group, so fewer patients have to get a placebo.
  • Imaging and pathology: read scans and tissue slides automatically to measure outcomes.
  • Documents: draft trial protocols and regulatory submissions. Unglamorous, but a big job and a natural fit for LLMs.
  • Safety monitoring: mine adverse-event reports for side effects nobody has noticed yet.
3.8

Drug repurposing: new uses for old drugs

An approved drug has already passed human safety testing. If it turns out to work for a second disease, you can skip years of development. AI approaches this in two main ways.

1. Knowledge graph reasoning

In early 2020, BenevolentAI searched its knowledge graph for approved drugs that might block the coronavirus from entering cells and also calm the immune overreaction. It picked out baricitinib, a rheumatoid arthritis drug. Trials confirmed a benefit. It received emergency authorization for COVID-19 in late 2020 and full FDA approval in 2022.

2. Signature matching

A disease pushes some genes up and others down; this pattern is its "signature." Databases such as LINCS/CMap record how thousands of drugs shift gene activity in cells. Look for a drug whose signature is the mirror image of the disease's.

Section 4

Hype and reality

Knowing what works and what doesn't is what makes a pitch sound credible.

AreaMaturityWhy

Track record so far

Dozens of AI-discovered molecules have entered clinical trials, which is a real milestone. A few have reported encouraging mid-stage results. Some of the best-known AI drug companies have also had major setbacks, cut programs, or merged (Recursion and Exscientia combined in 2024).

The fair summary: AI has clearly sped up early discovery, in some reported cases cutting the time from target to candidate molecule from four or five years to one or two. There is not yet evidence that it improves clinical success rates, because too few AI-discovered drugs have finished late-stage trials to tell.

This is the main structural criticism. Finding hits ten times faster doesn't fix 90% clinical failure, unless the candidates are actually better (more likely to work in people), not just found sooner. Whether AI can do that is the open question in the field. It's also the strongest framing for a project: use AI to make better decisions earlier, not just to go faster.

Appendix A

Glossary

Target
The protein (usually) that a drug is meant to act on.
Ligand
Any molecule that binds a protein. A drug is a ligand.
Binding pocket
A groove on the protein's surface where a ligand can sit.
Potency / IC50
How little drug you need for an effect. IC50 is the concentration that cuts the target's activity in half. Lower is more potent.
Selectivity
How well a drug hits its target without hitting others.
Hit
A molecule that shows any real activity against the target in a screen.
Lead
An improved hit that is promising enough to optimize seriously.
DMTA cycle
Design, make, test, analyze: the lab loop of lead optimization.
ADMET
Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity: what the body does to the drug, and what harm the drug might do.
hERG
A heart ion channel. Drugs that block it by accident can cause dangerous heart rhythms.
QSAR
Quantitative structure-activity relationship: predicting a molecule's properties from its structure. The original "ML for chemistry".
SMILES
A way to write a molecule as a line of text, e.g. aspirin is CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O.
Fingerprint
A fixed-length list of bits marking which small chemical patterns a molecule contains.
Docking
Software that fits a molecule into a protein pocket and scores the fit.
Druggable
Possible to target with a drug, usually meaning the protein has a suitable pocket.
GWAS
Genome-wide association study: finding genetic variants that are more common in people with a disease.
Biomarker
Something you can measure, such as a gene, a protein level or a scan feature, that shows disease state or drug response.
Modality
The kind of drug: small molecule, antibody, peptide, nucleic acid, cell therapy.
Repurposing
Finding a new disease for an already approved drug.
Phase 1 / 2 / 3
Clinical trial stages: safety in a small group, then whether it works and at what dose, then large confirmatory trials.
