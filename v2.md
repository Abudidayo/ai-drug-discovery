Here's the major difference from the previous plan.

Open Targets becomes the evidence source, not the decision-maker.

A trained graph model attempts to learn relationships that are not explicitly present in the evidence. The Model jury then challenges the predictions instead of merely repeating the score used to generate them.

We must still check whether TxGNN's disease and drug identifiers can be mapped to OxHack's sources. Its published coverage does not guarantee support for every Open Targets disease, and its predictions are not evidence of clinical effectiveness.
3. The scientifically interesting part: make the Model jury try to falsify predictions
Suppose a graph model ranks Candidate A highly for a disease.

Instead of asking the jury, "Is Candidate A promising?", ask it to investigate:
- Is there independent evidence linking the candidate's targets to the disease?
- Does the candidate inhibit or activate the target in a biologically useful direction?
- Are the reported effects plausible at achievable drug exposure?
- Are there contradictory experimental results or major safety concerns?
- What evidence is missing that would prevent us from recommending further investigation?
This changes the jury's function from ranking generator to critical scientific reviewer.

The jury should initially receive the candidate evidence without the graph model's numerical ranking. We could then compare its judgment against the graph prediction.

That doesn't make the jury an objective authority. We would still need to verify its citations and evaluate whether it improves decisions.
4. The real research contribution would be evaluation
This is where OxHack could become much more technically serious.

Your team lead's existing plan.md already includes an unusually useful starting point: evidence-masking modes, known-answer backtests, random and popularity baselines, Hits@5, MRR and confidence intervals. I would preserve these rather than discard them.

I'd expand the evaluation to answer four separate questions:
Research question	Experiment
Can the graph model generalise?	Test diseases with treatment relationships withheld during training
Does it outperform simpler methods?	Compare against Open Targets-derived scores, shared-target counts and popularity baselines
Does the jury contribute value?	Compare graph-only rankings with evidence-reviewed rankings on the same candidate pools
Can we trust the explanations?	Verify supporting citations, drug mechanisms and contradictions against source records

A proper historical experiment must also restrict all model inputs to the evidence available at its selected cutoff. Simply hiding the known answer from the LLM while retaining it in the graph or other features would not be sufficient.

And because unknown drug–disease relationships aren't necessarily false, we'd need carefully designed evaluation negatives.
5. Include ablation studies
This is something I'd particularly prioritise if you want the project to demonstrate research depth.

An ablation study removes part of a system to measure whether it actually contributes anything.

For OxHack, compare these configurations:
Configuration	Purpose
Open Targets-derived baseline	Establish performance without learned graph predictions
Graph model only	Measure what graph learning adds
Graph model + mechanism checks	Test whether biological reasoning improves prioritisation
Graph model + jury	Measure the jury's contribution
Full pipeline	Evaluate the combined workflow

For example, if the graph model outperforms the baseline but the Model jury consistently worsens ranking performance, that's an important finding.

We shouldn't disguise that failure with an impressive-looking consensus score.

A strong research project explains what works, what fails, and why.

7. The honest feasibility boundary
There's still a substantial engineering risk here.

TxGNN's public implementation uses an older Python/DGL software stack. The authors provide model-loading instructions and example weights, but we have not yet demonstrated that those weights can be used with your team's Open Targets identifiers or deployed on its infrastructure.

I'd give the graph component a strict feasibility checkpoint before treating it as a required dependency.

If TxGNN isn't viable, we can still build a technically rigorous alternative: a smaller graph-based ranking model or network-propagation method evaluated against held-out data. That would be a real prediction method, not simply asking LLMs to reorder Open Targets' list.
My recommendation for the project's identity
Instead of describing OxHack as:

An application where multiple AI models rank drugs from Open Targets.

I'd position it as:

An evidence-grounded drug-repurposing research platform that investigates graph-learning predictions, tests their biological plausibility, and measures their reliability through reproducible experiments.

The important research contribution isn't necessarily inventing a completely new neural-network architecture. It can be a rigorously engineered and evaluated system that establishes whether graph prediction plus independent scientific evidence analysis actually improves candidate prioritisation.

That's the direction I'd be comfortable defending in a technical interview, a research presentation or an AstraZeneca application.

I would make one decision before rewriting the team's plan again: test TxGNN as the primary zero-shot graph-model candidate, with KG-Bench's RGCN retained as a baseline on supported data—not as the compulsory engine. This is the first point where the scientific literature offers a credible route around the coverage objection without giving up on real machine learning.
